Sun, Earth & Moon

Why days, seasons and Moon phases happen.

↓

Meet the system

Not to scale

The Sun lights everything. Earth goes around the Sun once a year, spinning as it goes, and the Moon goes around Earth.

Real sizes won't fit on a screen: the Sun is about 109 Earths wide, the Moon is about 30 Earth-widths away, and the Sun is 150 million km away.

1. Day & night

Earth spins on its axis. The half facing the Sun has day; the other half has night. The red pin is a place at 40°N — watch it move through sunrise, noon, sunset and midnight.

One spin takes 24 hours. At the equator the ground moves at about 1670 km/h.

2. Seasons

Earth's axis is tilted 23.4° and always points the same way in space. In June the north leans toward the Sun: the Sun climbs higher and days are longer. In December it leans away.

At 40°N that means about 15 h of daylight in June and 9 h in December. The southern hemisphere gets the opposite season.

Myth: "Summer is when Earth is closer to the Sun." Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January — distance doesn't cause seasons.

3. Moon phases

The Sun always lights half of the Moon. As the Moon goes around Earth, we see a changing share of that lit half: new, crescent, quarter, gibbous, full and back.

One cycle takes 29.5 days. It isn't Earth's shadow — that only happens during a lunar eclipse.

4. Eclipses

If the Moon goes between us and the Sun every month, why isn't there an eclipse every month? Because the Moon's orbit is tilted 5.1°, so most months it passes above or below the Sun's line.

Eclipses only happen near the two points where the orbits cross — about twice a year, in "eclipse seasons". A solar eclipse is the Moon's shadow on Earth; a lunar eclipse is Earth's shadow on the Moon.

The tilt is drawn 3× larger so you can see it. Real eclipse seasons drift ~19 days earlier each year; this model keeps them fixed.
