How a 4-Stroke Engine Works

Scroll to take the engine apart, one stroke at a time.

↓

Meet the parts

  • Piston slides up and down inside the cylinder.
  • Connecting rod links the piston to the crankshaft.
  • Crankshaft turns the piston's up-and-down motion into rotation.
  • Intake valve lets the fuel–air mixture in.
  • Exhaust valve lets burnt gas out.
  • Camshaft pushes the valves open at exactly the right moment.
  • Spark plug ignites the mixture.

1. Intake

The intake valve opens and the piston moves down, drawing a fresh fuel–air mixture (blue) into the cylinder.

Bore 86 mm × stroke 86 mm. Displacement = π/4 × bore² × stroke ≈ 0.5 L per cylinder.

2. Compression

Both valves close and the piston moves back up, squeezing the mixture into a tiny space at the top.

Compression ratio 10:1 — the mixture is squeezed to a tenth of its volume, heating it up.

3. Power

The spark ignites the mixture. The burning gas expands and drives the piston down hard, turning the crankshaft.

Only 1 of the 4 strokes produces power; the flywheel carries the engine through the other three.

4. Exhaust

The exhaust valve opens and the piston rises again, pushing the burnt gas (grey) out. Then the cycle starts over.

One full cycle takes 2 crank turns, so the camshaft turns at half crank speed.

Real engines open the valves slightly early and close them late — called valve overlap; this model keeps them separate for clarity.

From 1 to 4 cylinders

Car engines usually have several cylinders sharing one crankshaft, each at a different point in the cycle.

An inline-4 fires in the order 1-3-4-2: a power stroke every 180° of crank rotation, so power delivery is much smoother.
